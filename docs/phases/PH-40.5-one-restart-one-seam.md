## 1. Objective

Two things the Human Owner asked about seams, answered with code and with a
measurement: every clean restart wrote **two** seams per asset
([#23](https://github.com/NovaHub2026/otcv6/issues/23)), and the seam rate the
Owner saw (1,762 in one day) was measured on a machine running builds, test
suites and agents beside the engine.

## 2. One restart, one seam (#23)

`start()` resumes the markets one after another; on a full record that took 26 s
on the live venue. A market seamed at the boot opens at the clock of its resume,
so the markets resumed first were already past their 15 s catch-up bound when the
first pass ran, and ADR-0020 reopened them — a second seam per asset, and
`otc_market_reopenings_total` reading 30 after every clean boot.

**The fix.** After every market is resumed and primed, and before the first pass,
each market that booted on a seam is opened again at the clock
(`VenueService.#rearmBootSeams`). It has published nothing since its seam, so this
writes nothing: the same resume is taken again, and because `resumeMarket` only
reads, it comes back at the same price and the **same reserved sequence** — the
one the feed has already declared to any client that connected meanwhile. That
property is checked, not assumed: a second resume that disagrees is logged and
left as the boot took it. Everything is read first and every market rebuilt
against one clock reading, so a slow store cannot give the first-rebuilt markets
the same head start on their bound.

## 3. Guards

`apps/api/src/bootSeam.test.ts` is the test the issue asked for: a boot whose
`start()` is slow on purpose — a store whose every read costs the clock nine
seconds, three markets read twice over — then thirty seconds of passes. It
asserts one seam per asset, **no automatic reopening**, and that each market
resumed at exactly the sequence its boot declared.

**The record alone could not have caught it.** Before the fix the test failed on
the reopening count, not on the seams: the reopened market had published nothing
between its two openings, so the record holds one gap either way. What moved was
the reserved sequence — a whole lease past the one the feed had announced — and
the counter an operator watches. So the guard asserts those, not the gap count.

| Plant                                                    | What went red                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| no re-arm at the end of `start()` (the reproduction)     | 1 reopening: the first market resumed fell past 15 s   |
| the re-arm re-reads the slow store market by market      | 1 reopening: the read-first design is what prevents it |
| the re-arm reads everything but keeps the boot's markets | 3 reopenings                                           |

## 4. The seam rate on an engine with its own host

Recorded in [`SEAM-RATE-2026-09-27.md`](../evidence/SEAM-RATE-2026-09-27.md):
the final build alone for 30 minutes, then with 16 and 32 busy processes on 16
cores. **No reopening in any of it** — the worst gap between passes was 1.6 s
against a 15 s bound. The host then slept twice while a broken measurement
script hung, and each sleep produced exactly one seam per market, recovered with
no operator. Contention does not seam this engine; a process stopped outright
does, and a server that does not sleep does not stop it.

## 6. What the live venue found after approval

The venue on 7300 was restarted from `main` after the phase merged, after
fourteen hours down. Every market seamed once at the boot and was re-armed at the
end of `start()`, as built — and **39 s later every one was reopened anyway**:
the first pass came that late, and the re-arm had assumed it would follow at
once. What held the pass was not established (nothing was logged; a boot under
the CPU profiler did not reproduce it). Two things were, and both are fixed:

- **A late first pass must not reopen a seamed boot.** A market that booted on a
  seam has published nothing since it — PH-39's "awaiting its first tick" — so it
  is now marked so at the boot, and past its bound it is re-armed, writing no
  second seam and counted as a re-arm, not a reopening. Guard: the first pass
  forty seconds late; planted away, it reopens.
- **The record's retention trim cost 17% of the process.** A CPU profile of the
  live venue attributed 6.7 s of 40 s — all of its SQLite time — to `trim`, run
  on every checkpoint: a `COUNT(*)` and an `OFFSET` walk over each asset's
  250,000-row window every five seconds, synchronous, between the awaits of the
  pass it delays. An asset is now trimmed once it has appended a hundredth of its
  window since its last trim, and always on a process's first checkpoint; the
  record holds at most 1% past its window, never less. Guards: a plant that
  trimmed on every checkpoint, and one that trimmed only once, both fail. The
  first survived a window of 400, whose hundredth is fewer ticks than one
  checkpoint appends — the test now uses 2,000.
- **What was not established is now measured.** Passes of ten to sixteen seconds
  were seen after some boots on this machine while the event loop kept
  answering; the worst reopened every market once, as a genuine second stall. A
  clean boot, quick or seamed, stayed under 0.4 s; CPU saturation at twice the
  cores stayed under 1.6 s; a disk writing 4 GB with `fsync` stayed under 0.2 s;
  and a CPU profile of a calm minute showed only the trim above. The bad boots
  coincided with the launcher rebuilding the panel beside the engine, but a cause
  was not isolated. So the pass now times its four phases — advance, record,
  publish, checkpoint — exports the slowest of each since boot
  (`otc_pass_*_max_seconds`) and counts passes over five seconds
  (`otc_slow_passes_total`), and logs a `SLOW PASS` with its breakdown at most
  once a minute. Guards: a checkpoint made slow on purpose must be named as the
  phase in both; three plants watched failing. The phases are four gauges, not a
  label, because INV-010's guard allows no label but an asset id.
- **The instrumentation named it on its first long-downtime boot.** After an
  hour's outage the first checkpoint took 12.1 s — **the trim 11.3 s**, the
  state-file saves 0.5 s, the candle history 0.3 s — while a two-minute outage
  gave 0.67 s. The first version of the amortised trim paid every market's trim
  on a process's first checkpoint, and each is a `COUNT` and an `OFFSET` walk
  over a quarter-million rows: warm, cheap; with the record's pages evicted
  during a long outage, eleven seconds in one pass. After the fourteen-hour
  outage that pass ran past the bound, which is the 39-second stall above and
  the second opening of every market. Now each market owes one trim at a
  process's start — so a record cannot grow across restarts — and a checkpoint
  pays at most one of them, besides the markets that have appended a hundredth
  of their window. Guards: a plant that paid them all at once, and one that owed
  none, both fail.
