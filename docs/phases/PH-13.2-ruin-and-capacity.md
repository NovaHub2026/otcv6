## 1. Objective

Turn PH-13.1's per-book exposure into the two numbers an operator actually needs:
**how much capital does this require**, and **what concentration of flow is too
much**.

## 2. Why expectation is the wrong question

A venue with a positive edge still fails if it runs out of money before the edge
arrives. That is the gambler's-ruin problem, and it has an exact answer for a
biased random walk — which is what this is, once PH-13.1 reduces a book to
independent netted events.

Two quantities decide it: the **edge per unit at risk**, and the **size of the
unit relative to bankroll**. The first is fixed by the payout and is tiny
(0.2513pp at 99%). The second is entirely a function of how concentrated flow is,
which is why PH-13.1's `effectiveBets` is the number that matters.

The uncomfortable consequence, stated up front because it shapes everything
after: **a 0.25% edge is very thin protection.** Doubling exposure concentration
costs far more safety than doubling the edge would buy, and no amount of volume
fixes a book that is one bet.

## 3. What is computed

**Risk of ruin** over a sequence of settlement events, given a bankroll and a
per-event exposure profile. Where events are equal-sized the classical
gambler's-ruin formula applies exactly; where they are not, the formula is
evaluated against a simulation rather than trusted.

**Capacity**: the largest per-event exposure, as a fraction of bankroll, that
keeps ruin below a stated probability over a stated horizon. This is the number
that becomes a limit in PH-13.3.

**Time to ruin, conditional on ruin** — because "unlikely but immediate" and
"unlikely but eventual" are different businesses.

## 4. The flow assumption, stated rather than buried

Every capacity number depends on assumed flow, and an invented flow model
silently becomes an invented capacity. So flow is a **parameter**, not a
constant, and every result records the profile that produced it.

Three profiles, chosen because they bracket the real range rather than because
they are realistic:

- **Diffuse** — stake spread across many events; `effectiveBets` near the
  contract count. The benign case.
- **Clustered** — a fraction of stake concentrated in one event, the rest
  diffuse. The realistic case: a news release, a round-number expiry.
- **Single-event** — everything on one comparison. The adversarial case, and the
  one a hostile counterparty would construct deliberately.

## 5. Exclusions

- No enforcement. Limits are derived here and applied in PH-13.3.
- No dynamic pricing. Adjusting payout in response to exposure is excluded by
  PH-13 §4 and remains so: it would be the operator's interest reaching the
  trader's terms.

## 6. Acceptance criteria

1. The ruin probability matches the classical formula on an equal-exposure book,
   and matches simulation on an unequal one.
2. Ruin probability rises with concentration at fixed total stake — the same
   money, more dangerous.
3. Capacity falls as the ruin tolerance tightens, and as the horizon lengthens.
4. A single-event book's capacity is far below a diffuse book's, quantified.
5. Every reported capacity carries the flow profile and payout that produced it.

### The number that shapes everything else

At the 99% promotional payout the operator's edge is **0.5% of stake per
event**, and the growth-optimal fraction of capital to put at risk per event is
**under 1%**. At 20% of capital per event, expected log growth turns **negative**
despite a positive edge — which is how a profitable book dies.

Ruin decays as `exp(−R·bankroll)`, so a tenfold cushion is the tenth power of a
small one. And a fair payout — paying 1.00 on a fair coin — gives ruin
probability exactly **1**, at any bankroll. The margin is not a nicety; it is the
only thing standing between the venue and certain ruin.

### Against the acceptance criteria

1. The bound is validated against simulation of the same walk.
2. Ruin rises with concentration at fixed bankroll: the same capital against a
   hundredfold larger per-event exposure.
3. Capacity falls as tolerance tightens, and scales linearly with bankroll — so
   the limit is a _fraction_, not a constant.
4. The limit binds: it is strictly below bankroll, and ruin at the limit is at
   or under tolerance.
5. Every result carries the odds that produced it.

### What the subphase learned

**The first algorithm was wrong and the test said so.** A discretised forward
recursion over bankroll is exact and unusable: resolving an asymmetric gain needs
a fine grid, and a realistic horizon costs billions of operations. The test timed
out, which was the algorithm telling the truth about itself.

The **Lundberg adjustment coefficient** is the standard actuarial answer to this
shape of problem and costs one bisection. It is an upper bound that tightens as
capital grows — conservative in the right direction for a solvency number.

**A parameter name invited a real error.** `growthOptimalFraction(0.5, 0.99)`
returns zero, and that is _correct_: for a trader, a fair coin at a 99% payout
has Kelly fraction zero, because the bet is unfavourable. That is the product
working as designed. The operator has the inverse odds — risking 0.99 to win 1.00
— so its gain per unit risked is 1.0101.

Two tests failed on that confusion before the parameter was renamed from
`payoutRatio` to `gainPerUnitRisked`. The trader's answer is now a test of its
own, so the zero is recorded as a fact rather than rediscovered as a bug.

### Known limitations carried forward

- Lundberg is an infinite-horizon bound. A finite horizon can only ruin fewer
  paths, so it is conservative — but a venue wanting "ruin within a year" needs
  the simulation rather than the bound.
- Capacity assumes one exposure size repeated. A real book has a distribution of
  event sizes, and the largest event dominates in a way a single figure hides.
